Preface

This book is an introduction to the C language, and it is also an introduction to proven, a C library I wrote. There is a reason it serves both purposes.

Before you can recommend a tool, you have to show why it is needed. The problems proven addresses live in the subtle corners of C — the limits of representation, the rules of conversion, the lifetime of storage, the areas the standard declines to promise anything about. To those who have not seen those corners, proven looks like an unnecessary contraption. To those who know them well, on the other hand, their own ways of handling those problems already exist, and there is little reason to reach for proven.

So I decided to explain the problems first and the library afterwards. With a first-time reader in mind, the book begins with how a computer is built and goes on through types and flow, pointers and arrays, the lifetime of storage and the standard library. Having read those earlier parts you may well decide you do not need proven — and that is a fine outcome too. Either way, they will have helped you understand C better and use it more safely.

In truth the library and the book start from the same place and aim at the same thing: to lift the way C is written, and the habits around it, up to what our time calls for. The book supplies the underlying knowledge; the library proposes new coding habits. They only take different roads to the same goal.

C has a long history, and in it are all sorts of scars and contortions and serious deliberations, each an attempt to get past the real limits and difficulties of its time. Tracing those marks has been an interesting and enjoyable thing to do, so adding my own effort to them was the natural next step. The purpose is the one just described, but that proven exists, and that this book exists, comes in the end less from necessity than from a liking for C; I will not pretend otherwise.

This book runs well past 1,300 pages and is likely to grow. I did not try to cut it down, and that is deliberate. The subtle places of C breed misunderstanding when they are put briefly — a rule squeezed into one line loses its conditions, and a rule that has lost its conditions easily becomes a wrong rule. Explaining without room for misunderstanding means writing the conditions and the counterexamples down together, and then it cannot be short. So I decided not to worry about length and to write as much as is needed. In return, I have made paths so that you need not read it all — the shortcut paths by level, right after this, are for that.

My thanks go to everyone who reads this. That someone spends their time reading my work and adding their thoughts to it is always a happy thing.

What this book intends, and how to use it#

Reading alone will not grow your ability to program. The body of this book sets out a situation, asks a question, and answers it. What it intends is that the reader end up with a firm grounding in C, and with a feel and an eye for the traps and dangers built into it.

So that you can check that feel for yourself, there is an exercise chapter at the end of each part, with the answers gathered at the back in “Answers to the exercises”; each exercise links to its answer. The exercises are gathered at the end of each part rather than mixed into the body so as not to break the flow of reading a chapter — while reading, follow the explanation; having finished a part, look back with its exercises. Most of them show wrong code and ask what is wrong, why, and how to fix it. How to use them is set out below, in “A request to learners”.

What the exercises train, though, is an eye for what is wrong. Standing in front of an empty file and breaking a problem into pieces, digging out the name of a function that will not come to mind, clearing the errors a compiler has poured out one by one, narrowing down the cause when the output is not what you expected, until one program is built to the end — that ability is something else again. It grows only by writing code yourself. This book does not do that work for you, and I will not claim it can. That part has to continue in some other course of practice, or in small programs of your own. Appendix P points to places to find practice. This is a book for understanding, not a book for making something quickly.

Even if that drill is done outside this book, I think the book keeps its place, because C has almost no safety net. In many languages a mistake such as an out-of-bounds access or a use of freed memory is reported on the spot by the runtime; C mostly accepts it and returns a plausible answer — which is to say that a wrong understanding survives without any signal that it is wrong. So moving your hands first, on thin ground, makes the drill expensive. The same thing takes more time and more trial and error to learn, and worse, a misconception hardens along the way. A hardened misconception becomes a bug later, and sometimes a security vulnerability. A firm foundation cannot do that drill for you, but it makes it far cheaper.

The first program comes late. Hello, world does not appear until Part III. Until then the book lays the groundwork — how a computer is built, how storage is divided, how numbers and letters are represented. There is a reason it comes late. Parts I and II are not background thrown in beside the subject; they carry weight of their own, and I think they are the most valuable part of this book. Most of what C looks like comes from what those two parts are about — how a machine handles memory, what can and cannot be represented, and what translation does. So to a reader who is short of time I would say this: skip everything else if you must, but read Parts I and II. And once you have, a fair number of the questions waiting further on — why unsigned integers wrap and why signed overflow must not be expected to, why the end of a string is invisible, why the compiler does not do what you wrote — will already have given you a lead. A reader in a hurry to build something may of course find this order slow-going. Such a reader may read chapter 16, hello world, first and come back to chapter 1. This book was written to be read in order, but take from it what suits your level and your purpose.

What the book intends is a firm foundation: not a quick result but an understanding that lasts. Skills picked up from other books and courses sit far more steadily on top of it. The gap between someone who knows the answers to those questions, why a pointer is not simply a number, and what a compiler does and does not promise, and someone who does not, only widens with time.

Every example in the body has actually been compiled and run. The exercises do contain code that is wrong on purpose; a checker counts that separately as “deliberately broken fragments”. The printed output is not transcribed by hand: it is what the machine produced when the examples were run during the build. gcc is the base and every example passes under it; clang is used as a cross-check, but a few examples skip it. Three are examples whose whole point is the situation clang diagnoses (silencing the warning would blunt the lesson), two use GCC-only attributes, and one uses a C23 rule that older clang releases do not yet accept, so it is skipped on those releases only. What is skipped, and why, is written down in the repository.

One more disclosure. This book was written with AI as an assisting tool. What to cover and in what order, which principles the exposition follows, what to include and what to leave out — all of that I decided; the manuscript was written to those instructions and then reviewed by me. Technical claims were checked, as far as I could, against the standard and primary sources, and, as said above, the examples are verified by a machine that actually runs them on every build — which means that whether a human or an AI wrote it, no code printed as an example fails to run.

Errors will remain nonetheless. Those are mine, not the tool’s. If you find one, please tell me.

A request to instructors#

Assessment does two jobs. One is telling learners how far they have come so as to help their next step (formative assessment); the other is judging and comparing what has been learned (summative assessment and selection). Education’s first job is the former, and the latter should come after a fair chance to learn has been given. Yet the structure of a course leans easily toward the latter: leaving students to solve things on their own, in the name of their autonomy, and keeping those who manage it is the path that costs the teacher least.

So I ask instructors who use this book as a course text: please use its exercises for formative assessment — not as a tool for grading without enough explanation and teaching beforehand.

A course that hands out exercises without explanation, and treats students who cannot keep up as a matter of diligence or attitude, becomes an obstacle race rather than a learning process. Asking first whether a learning path that supports the learner’s development was provided before the judging is the job of the schools and instructors who run a curriculum. A failure to learn does not have causes in the learner alone.

The exercises in this book are mostly hard, and harder for someone learning for the first time. They were written to make a beginner wonder in directions they had not thought of, and to learn by reading the explanation in the answer — they assume, to some extent, that you will get them wrong or not solve them. There is research showing that trying first, getting it wrong, and then reading the explanation helps learning. That effect, though, holds when explanation and feedback follow promptly. Use these problems for judging before learning, and they measure not what was learned in the course but what students already knew when they came — the assessment fails to measure what it set out to, and its validity collapses.

And please do not put this book’s problems into an exam as they are. The answers are printed at the back of the same book, so such an exam measures not whether students learned but whether they reached the answers. An exam that sets the same problems every year is no different. If you design assessment, weigh its basic requirements together: validity, reliability, objectivity and practicality.

A request to learners#

There is something to ask of the learner, too.

You may have heard of the Dunning–Kruger effect. In 1999 Kruger and Dunning reported that the people who did a task worst overestimated their own skill the most. The curve that usually goes round — the beginner climbing a “peak of Mount Stupid” and then falling into a “valley of despair” — is not a figure from that paper but one that spread later, and how large the effect is, and why it arises, is still argued over by researchers. Still, the picture spread for a reason. Anyone who has learned anything has known the moment when knowing a little feels like knowing it best.

The depth and breadth of learning, and of reality itself, are always beyond what we imagine. This book, too, holds only a part of C, and only as much of it as could be checked on a few machines with a few compilers. So do not settle things too quickly; keep an open mind. When “that is just how C is” is on the tip of your tongue, first ask whether it is something the standard guarantees or something you saw once on your own machine. That is why this book has kept the two apart throughout.

And always keep in mind that you may be wrong. Never being wrong is not possible. What matters is to be ready, in advance, to catch an error when it happens and to repair it. The habits of this book — keeping warnings on, getting failure back as a value, leaving behind a test that catches a fixed defect again — are all that preparation. The same is true of me: the checks that find errors in this book have grown, one or two at a time, at the places where I was wrong.

Here is how I suggest using the exercises. First, without looking at the answer, write down your own — it is fine to be wrong. If you are stuck, before going to the answer, open the chapter the exercise points to again. Then read the answer, reading “why the mistake is easy to make” before the right answer. Solve the same exercise again a few days later, and you will see what you did not know the first time.

Last, and I ask this earnestly: be generous with other people’s mistakes and gaps. It is common for knowledge that was right at one stage to be corrected at the next — this very book corrects, later on, the simple picture of the machine it set up at the start, yet that earlier picture did its job at its stage. A wrong idea, too, usually has a good reason for being wrong in the way it is; that is why the “common misconception” boxes in this book start by writing the wrong idea down as it is. Keep your mind on why a misconception arises, and you can be generous both with others who are wrong and with yourself when you have thought wrongly.

So use knowledge for the practical value it gives and for what it does to your own thinking, not to rank people or to attack and belittle them. In a code review, what you point at is the code, not the person, and correcting what is wrong can be done perfectly well without putting anyone down. What that needs is flexible thinking and humility — and this one warning I want to give: the more you know, the easier both become to lose. More people using what they have learned that way is one more result this book hopes for.
