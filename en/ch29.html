Looking back

Chapter 21 only announced that “7 / 2 is not 3.5.” Using what chapter 6 taught, ask yourself — why can the result of dividing integer containers not be 3.5?

A. Because there is nowhere to hold 3.5. The value set of an integer type has 3 and 4 and nothing in between — so division between integers must give an integer, and the fractional part must be discarded one way or the other. The question is “which way”, and that rule is this chapter’s first section.

The need for this chapter, and its context

This pays the promise chapter 21 deferred with “division comes later”. The reason for deferring is now plain: the truth about integer division only makes sense once integer types (chapter 28) are known. The same goes for the bitwise operators — chapter 6’s concepts finally arrive as operators. It is the last of three steps: representation, type, operation.

By the end of this chapter

We keep the promise deferred in chapter 21 — the truth about integer division / and remainder %, including the rule for negative numbers. And the bit operations learned as concepts in chapter 6 join as C operators. The first step into conversions is taken here too.

The questions this chapter answers

  1. What happens if you divide by zero?

29.1 Division and remainder — which way the fraction is discarded#

The demonstration first.

examples-en/integer-ops/divmod.c

#include <stdio.h>

int main(void)
{
    printf(" 7 / 2 = %d,  7 %% 2 = %d\n", 7 / 2, 7 % 2);
    printf("-7 / 2 = %d, -7 %% 2 = %d\n", -7 / 2, -7 % 2);
    printf("invariant: (7/2)*2 + 7%%2 = %d\n", (7 / 2) * 2 + 7 % 2);

    printf("5 & 3 = %d, 5 | 3 = %d, 5 ^ 3 = %d\n", 5 & 3, 5 | 3, 5 ^ 3);
    printf("1 << 4 = %d\n", 1 << 4);
    return 0;
}

Output

 7 / 2 = 3,  7 % 2 = 1
-7 / 2 = -3, -7 % 2 = -1
invariant: (7/2)*2 + 7%2 = 7
5 & 3 = 1, 5 | 3 = 7, 5 ^ 3 = 6
1 << 4 = 16

For positive numbers it is as intuition says — 7 / 2 is 3, remainder 1. Where a rule is needed is negatives. C truncates toward zero — -7 / 2 is −3.5 discarded towards zero, giving −3, and the remainder is correspondingly −1. That is a different choice from “the quotient and remainder of mathematics” (where the remainder is always non-negative), so code using negative remainders causes accidents if it does not know the difference.

The mathematics. The quotient-remainder identity

Whatever the direction, there is one equation C always keeps:

(𝑎𝑏)×𝑏+(𝑎%𝑏)=𝑎

Quotient and remainder are defined as a pair so as to satisfy this equation — the demonstration’s last line is a check of it. That truncating toward zero makes the remainder’s sign follow the dividend (𝑎) is also a consequence of this equation. A line of history in addition — up to C89 the direction of discarding was allowed to differ by implementation, and C99 pinned it to “toward zero.” Chapter 6’s “where machines diverge the standard leaves a blank” is here another case of practice promoted to promise once the machines converged.

Q. What happens if you divide by zero?

A. Outside the contract — undefined behaviour. It is one of the rare cases where what is undefined in mathematics is undefined in C too, but the result is not as well-behaved as in mathematics: on many machines the program collapses on the spot, and under some optimisations stranger things happen (chapter 56). Checking for zero before dividing is the programmer’s job — and once we learn branching in chapter 32, we will be able to write that check in code.

29.2 Bit operations — chapter 6’s world, in C’s syntax#

The bit handling learned as concepts in chapter 6 joins as operators — AND &, OR |, XOR ^, complement ~, and the shifts << and >>. The latter part of the demonstration is a taste: 5 & 3 is 101 & 011 = 001, so 1; 5 | 3 is 111, so 7; and 1 << 4 is 24=16, exactly as chapter 6 promised.

The basic pattern in practice is exactly the shift plus mask foreshadowed in chapter 6 — push to the position you want (<<, >>) and keep only the bits you need (&). But take two rules along with it. First, do bit operations on unsigned types — shifts of signed numbers carry the traps seen in chapter 6 (a shift count at least the width, left-shifting a negative = outside the contract), so playing on unsigned or uint32_t is the safe practice. Second, & (bitwise AND) and && (logical AND, chapter 31) are completely different operators — one character changes the entire value.

29.3 The contracts the integer operators make#

Here the operators met so far are reviewed in terms of their contracts: what each takes, and where the contract ends (the full table is in appendix A).

Shifts have four grey zones, so they get their own table. These four have not changed across the editions of the standard, C23 included.

A common misconception. “C23 mandated two’s complement, so the negative-shift problem is gone”

Two’s complement representation was indeed mandated (chapter 6). The shift clause, however, was left alone — left-shifting a signed negative value is still outside the contract in C23, and right-shifting a negative value is still implementation-defined. That gcc and clang do an arithmetic shift is a promise of those implementations, not of the standard.

So this book’s rule stands whatever the edition — shift on unsigned types. If a signed value must be shifted, move it to unsigned, shift, and move it back; and always check 0 <= n < width, where the width is sizeof(x) * CHAR_BIT.

29.4 Conversion — crossing between containers#

With a family of types (chapter 28) comes a new question — what happens when containers of different kinds are mixed in a calculation? C’s answer is implicit conversion: the value in a smaller container is automatically widened into a larger one before the calculation (chapter 6’s sign extension is exactly what happens then), and when an integer meets a floating-point number the integer is converted to floating point. Mostly this does what you meant, but automatic also means invisible — comparisons mixing signed with unsigned in particular are a classic trap (a negative number turns into an enormous positive one), and a representative place where compiler warnings (-Wall -Wextra) protect you.

When you want the conversion stated, use the cast notation — (double)7 / 2 means “move 7 into a floating container and then divide”, giving 3.5. The full rules (integer promotion, the usual arithmetic conversions) are left as reference material for the next chapter (chapter 30) and the appendix; the text’s advice comes down to two rules: state the intent of a mixed calculation with a cast, and avoid comparisons that mix signs.

We have the containers of integers (chapter 28) and their operations (chapter 29). The next chapter is the conversion that happens silently when types mix; after that comes flow — starting with the values that compare and decide, the booleans.
